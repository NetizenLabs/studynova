# A Blank Cornell Page Is Easy. A Finished One Is the Skill.

Most students understand the Cornell layout in about thirty seconds: questions and keywords in the narrow left column, lecture notes in the wide right column, and a summary at the bottom. Then they open a blank page and freeze.

What belongs in each section? How much should you write? Should the cue column be filled in during the lecture or afterwards? And what does a summary look like when it is more than the first sentence of the notes copied again?

This article shows two complete examples: one from a biology lecture and one from a history lecture. They are intentionally different. Biology needs processes, locations and quantities in the right order. History needs causes, evidence and distinctions between a trigger and an underlying weakness.

Use the same habits explained in [The Cornell Notes Method](/blog/note-taking-methods), then print the free [Cornell Notes template](/printables/cornell-notes) and try the layout on your next lecture.

---

## The Three Rules Behind Both Examples

### 1. The notes column records the lecture, not a transcript

Write compressed phrases, arrows, diagrams described in words and worked distinctions. Leave space between ideas so you can add corrections after class.

### 2. The cue column is written mainly after the lecture

Turn each section of notes into a question, prompt or keyword. A good cue hides the answer: cover the right side and the cue should force you to explain something specific.

### 3. The summary is an answer, not a list of topics

In two to four sentences, answer: “What was this lecture really saying, and why does it matter?” Write it in your own words the same day, while the argument is still fresh.

# Example 1: Biology — Cellular Respiration

**Class:** Biology 101  
**Topic:** How cells release energy from glucose  
**Lecture goal:** Follow carbon, electrons and ATP through the stages of aerobic cellular respiration.

| Cue column: ask yourself | Notes column: what to record |
|---|---|
| **What is the overall job of cellular respiration?** | Cells transfer energy from glucose into ATP, the molecule used to power cellular work. Overall equation often written as: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + usable energy (ATP). The process happens in stages so energy is released in controlled steps rather than all at once. |
| **Where does glycolysis happen, and what comes out?** | **Glycolysis — cytosol.** One glucose molecule (6 carbons) is split into **2 pyruvate** molecules (3 carbons each). Net gain: **2 ATP** and **2 NADH**. Oxygen is not directly required for this stage. |
| **What happens to pyruvate before the citric acid cycle?** | **Pyruvate oxidation — mitochondrial matrix.** Each pyruvate loses one carbon as CO₂ and is converted into acetyl-CoA. This step produces **1 NADH per pyruvate** — so 2 NADH for the two pyruvate made from one glucose. |
| **What should I remember from one turn of the citric acid cycle?** | **Citric acid cycle / Krebs cycle — mitochondrial matrix.** For each acetyl-CoA: **3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP (counted like ATP), and 2 CO₂**. Because one glucose produces two acetyl-CoA, double those amounts for a per-glucose total. |
| **Why is oxygen essential if glycolysis can happen without it?** | **Oxidative phosphorylation — inner mitochondrial membrane.** NADH and FADH₂ deliver electrons to the electron transport chain. The chain powers proton pumping; ATP synthase uses the proton gradient to make most of the ATP. **Oxygen is the final electron acceptor** and combines with electrons and hydrogen ions to form water. Without oxygen, the chain backs up. |
| **What ATP total should I quote?** | Textbook totals vary because transport costs and shuttle systems differ. Many introductory courses teach **about 30–32 ATP per glucose** in eukaryotic cells. The important exam skill is to explain where the ATP comes from and why the yield is an estimate, not to pretend every cell produces an identical number. |
| **What is the most common mix-up?** | Do not confuse *where carbon goes* with *where energy carriers go*. Carbon leaves mainly as CO₂. NADH and FADH₂ carry electrons to the inner membrane. ATP is produced in small amounts early and in much larger amounts during oxidative phosphorylation. |

### Summary for the biology page

Cellular respiration releases energy from glucose in stages: glycolysis begins the breakdown in the cytosol, pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle complete the carbon breakdown in mitochondria while loading electron carriers, and oxidative phosphorylation uses oxygen to turn that stored electron energy into most of the cell’s ATP. If I can trace one glucose molecule’s carbons and explain oxygen’s role, I understand the lecture — not just the vocabulary.

### How this page would be used later

Cover the notes column. Read only the cues and answer aloud. Mark any cue you cannot answer — “Why is oxygen essential?” is a likely weak point — and schedule that section for the next review. This is the point where notes become [active recall](/blog/active-recall), not storage.

# Example 2: History — Why the Great Depression Became So Severe

**Class:** U.S. History  
**Topic:** From stock-market crash to nationwide depression  
**Lecture goal:** Separate the crash of 1929 from the deeper economic weaknesses that turned a downturn into the Great Depression.

| Cue column: ask yourself | Notes column: what to record |
|---|---|
| **Did the stock-market crash alone cause the Great Depression?** | No. The crash was a major shock and a symbol of collapsing confidence, but the lecture’s argument is that it exposed weaknesses already present in the economy. A strong answer should distinguish the **trigger** from the **conditions that made the crisis spread**. |
| **What weakness existed in farms and factories before 1929?** | **Overproduction and underconsumption.** Farms and factories could produce more than many households could buy. Income was unevenly distributed, so mass purchasing power did not keep pace with output. Unsold goods piled up, prices and profits fell, and employers cut production and jobs. |
| **How did speculation make the crash worse?** | Many investors bought shares with borrowed money — **buying on margin**. Rising prices made the loans look safe while the market climbed. When prices fell, investors had to sell quickly to repay loans, pushing prices down further and wiping out people who owned only a small fraction of the shares they controlled. Key dates: panic selling in late October 1929, including **Black Thursday (Oct. 24)** and **Black Tuesday (Oct. 29)**. |
| **Why did bank failures hurt people who never owned stock?** | Banks had made risky loans and investments. As borrowers defaulted and frightened depositors rushed to withdraw cash, **bank runs** forced many banks to close. Deposit insurance through the FDIC did not yet exist, so ordinary families could lose their savings. Failing banks also stopped lending, which starved businesses of credit. |
| **How did the gold standard limit the response?** | Countries committed to the gold standard feared losing gold reserves and often kept money tight or raised interest rates to defend their currency’s gold value. That made credit more expensive during a collapse in spending. The system also transmitted deflation and financial pressure between countries. |
| **How did trade policy deepen the global downturn?** | The **Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930** raised U.S. tariffs on many imports. Other countries retaliated with tariffs of their own. World trade shrank, hurting exporters and farmers and spreading unemployment beyond the United States. Historians debate the size of the tariff’s effect, but it clearly worsened international cooperation during the crisis. |
| **What evidence shows how severe the depression became?** | U.S. unemployment rose to roughly **25% in the early 1930s** on the commonly used historical estimates for the period. Industrial production and prices fell sharply, thousands of banks failed, and farm foreclosures and homelessness spread. These outcomes explain why the event is called a depression rather than an ordinary recession. |

### Summary for the history page

The crash of 1929 did not create every weakness in the American economy; it exposed them. Overproduction, weak consumer purchasing power, margin speculation, fragile banks, gold-standard constraints and collapsing international trade turned a financial shock into a long, worldwide depression. The strongest exam answers separate the crash as the trigger from the chain reaction that followed.

### How this page would be used later

Cover the notes and use each cue as a short-answer prompt. Then try one synthesis question that is not written on the page: “Which two causes best explain why the crisis spread beyond Wall Street?” If you cannot build an answer from the cues, the page needs a new cue — not more highlighting.

## What Makes These Examples “Good”?

### The cues are questions, not copied headings

“Citric acid cycle” tells you what a section is about. “What should I remember from one turn of the citric acid cycle?” tells you what to retrieve. The second version creates a quiz.

### The notes compress instead of transcribe

Notice the arrows, bold distinctions and short explanations. A Cornell page should help you reconstruct the lecture. It does not need every sentence the professor said.

### Numbers have a job

The biology example does not list ATP numbers randomly; it explains what each stage produces and warns that totals vary. The history example uses dates and the unemployment estimate as evidence, not trivia.

### The summaries make a claim

Each summary can be read without the rest of the page and still communicate the lecture’s central idea. If your summary says only “We learned about respiration and the Depression,” it is a label, not a summary.

### The page is designed for a second encounter

The method continues after class. Revisit the cues later that day, again after a few days and again before the exam. That review rhythm pairs naturally with [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition): every return visit makes the next one faster.

## Try It on Your Next Lecture

Use this 20-minute after-class routine:

1. **Clean up the notes — 5 minutes.** Fill gaps, label diagrams and correct anything unclear.
2. **Write the cues — 7 minutes.** One strong question for each major section.
3. **Recite once — 5 minutes.** Cover the notes and answer the hardest three cues aloud.
4. **Write the summary — 3 minutes.** Explain the lecture’s main point without looking back.
5. **Schedule the review.** Add the page to tomorrow’s or the weekend’s study block in the [free Weekly Planner](/printables/weekly-planner).

Do not wait until you have a perfect set of lecture notes to start. Use one real lecture, one page and one honest summary. A finished Cornell page is not supposed to look like artwork — it is supposed to make the second, third and fourth review dramatically easier than the first.
