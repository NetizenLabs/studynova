# Seven Days Is Enough — If Every Day Has a Different Job

The last week before an exam usually fails in one of two ways. Some students try to “study everything” every day and finish the week exhausted but unsure what they actually know. Others keep rereading the easiest chapters because those pages feel familiar, then meet the hard questions for the first time in the exam hall.

A useful seven days needs a sequence. Early days build coverage and expose gaps. Middle days force retrieval and timed practice. The final days repair the highest-value weaknesses and then taper so your brain is rested enough to use what it knows.

This plan assumes **one major exam in seven days** and about **three to five focused hours per day**. If you work, commute or have classes, shrink the number of sessions — do not replace sleep with extra sessions. Put the blocks on the [free Weekly Planner](/printables/weekly-planner) at the start of Day 1 so the plan survives contact with your real week.

For the wider framework behind this week, read [The Ultimate 7-Day Exam Revision System](/blog/exam-revision-system). This article turns that framework into the daily work.

---

## Before Day 1: Gather the Raw Materials

Spend 20 minutes assembling:

- The syllabus, topic list or professor’s exam guide
- Lecture notes, slides and assigned readings
- Problem sets, quizzes and homework with solutions
- At least two past papers or sample exams, if available
- A blank topic tracker
- Your exam date, time, location and permitted materials

Do not organize these into beautiful folders for an hour. You need them findable, not decorative.

### Make a topic inventory

List every topic that could appear. Beside each one, add four columns:

| Topic | Exam weight or likelihood | Confidence now | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
|  | High / Medium / Low | Red / Yellow / Green | Quiz, problem set or “no evidence yet” |

Confidence must be based on **evidence**, not familiarity. If you have never answered a question on the topic without looking, it is not green — even if the chapter feels easy to read.

That distinction is the engine of this plan. As [Active Recall](/blog/active-recall) explains, recognition is not the same skill as producing an answer under exam conditions.

## The Seven Days at a Glance

| Day | Main job | Finished output |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Map and diagnose | Ranked topic list and first weak-spot list |
| Day 2 | Build the core | Notes, examples and questions for high-value topics |
| Day 3 | Retrieve, don’t reread | Completed recall rounds and updated confidence ratings |
| Day 4 | Test under pressure | One marked past paper and an error log |
| Day 5 | Repair the leaks | Corrected errors and a second pass at weak topics |
| Day 6 | Simulate the exam | Timed mock, grade estimate and final repair list |
| Day 7 | Taper and prepare | Short review, packed bag and a protected night’s sleep |

---

## Day 1: Map the Exam and Find the Gaps

**Goal:** Leave the day knowing exactly what the exam can test and where you are weakest.

### Session 1: Build the map — 60 to 90 minutes

Work through the syllabus and divide the course into topics small enough to practise. “Chapter 7” is too broad. “Calculate equilibrium concentrations” or “Explain the causes of the bank panic” is a topic you can test.

Mark each topic:

- **Red:** I cannot explain it or solve a typical problem without help.
- **Yellow:** I understand it, but my answers are slow, incomplete or unreliable.
- **Green:** I have answered questions correctly without notes.

### Session 2: Take a diagnostic pass — 60 minutes

Close your notes and attempt a mixed set of questions, problems or blank-page explanations. Use an old quiz, end-of-chapter questions or one section of a past paper.

You are not trying to earn a good score today. You are collecting information.

### Session 3: Rank the week — 30 minutes

Give first priority to topics that are both **likely to be tested** and **currently red or yellow**. A low-weight green topic does not need three more hours because it feels pleasant.

End Day 1 with no more than five priority topics for Day 2.

---

## Day 2: Build the Core You Will Keep Retrieving

**Goal:** Understand the highest-value material well enough to practise it without the textbook open beside you.

Work in focused blocks of 40 to 50 minutes, followed by real breaks. For each priority topic:

1. Read or watch the explanation once, actively: predict examples, question definitions and connect the idea to earlier material.
2. Close the source and write a one-page explanation from memory.
3. Reopen the source and correct what you missed in a different color.
4. Create five to ten recall prompts: questions, flashcards or problem types.
5. Work one example slowly, narrating why each step is legal or necessary.

This is where [Spaced Repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition) starts. A topic first learned on Day 2 should return briefly on Day 3, again on Day 5 and once more on Day 7. You are not “finishing” topics today; you are creating memories strong enough to be tested.

### What to do with a topic that makes no sense

Do not spend three silent hours staring at it. Change the input:

- Find the prerequisite idea you missed.
- Watch a worked example and pause before each step.
- Explain the simplest version out loud.
- Ask a classmate, tutor or instructor one specific question.
- Move to the next topic and return after a break.

“Specific” matters. “I don’t get thermodynamics” is hard to answer. “Why does the sign change when work is done on the system?” can be fixed in minutes.

---

## Day 3: Switch From Input to Retrieval

**Goal:** Make your brain produce answers, not merely recognize them.

By Day 3, at least two-thirds of your study time should be closed-book work.

### A Day 3 session that works

- **10 minutes:** Blank-page recall of yesterday’s topics.
- **25 minutes:** Flashcards or cue questions; say answers out loud before checking.
- **30 minutes:** Mixed practice problems — do not do ten nearly identical questions in a row.
- **10 minutes:** Check, correct and record every error.
- **5 minutes:** Rerate the topics red, yellow or green based on what happened.

Mixed practice feels harder because you must first decide *which method or idea applies*. That decision is part of the exam skill. Ten similar problems in a row quietly tell you the method before you begin.

End the day with an error log. Use four columns:

| Question or topic | What I did | Why it failed | Rule I will use next time |
|---|---|---|---|

“Careless mistake” is not a complete diagnosis. Did you misread the units, skip a condition, recall the wrong formula or run out of time? Each cause needs a different repair.

---

## Day 4: Take the First Past Paper Seriously

**Goal:** Discover how your knowledge behaves under time pressure.

If a past paper is available, complete it in one sitting:

- Use the real time limit.
- Put your phone in another room.
- Do not pause to look up answers.
- Mark questions you guessed, even if you chose correctly.
- If you get stuck, move on the way you would in the real exam.

### Mark it like an examiner, not a friend

Score the paper, then sort every lost mark into one of four groups:

1. **Knowledge gap:** I did not know the material.
2. **Method error:** I knew the topic but chose the wrong process.
3. **Execution error:** I set it up correctly but calculated, spelled or explained it badly.
4. **Time error:** I could have answered it with more time or better pacing.

Spend the rest of Day 4 only on the highest-value group. If most losses are knowledge gaps, return to Day 2 work for those topics. If they are pacing losses, more rereading will not solve them — you need timed practice.

If your course has no past papers, build a substitute from problem sets, textbook questions and the professor’s sample problems. Set a time limit based on the number and difficulty of the questions.

---

## Day 5: Repair, Re-test and Cover the Remaining Topics

**Goal:** Turn Day 4’s information into points.

Start with retrieval of the topics you studied on Days 2 and 3. Then divide the day into three parts:

### Part 1: Repair the top weaknesses

Choose the two topics that cost the most marks. Learn the missing idea, work a guided example, then solve a fresh problem without help. The repair is not complete until you can succeed on a question you have not just copied.

### Part 2: Cover what remains

Work through remaining yellow topics and any medium-weight material not yet touched. Keep explanations short and question-heavy. This is not the day to rewrite the entire textbook in neater handwriting.

### Part 3: Start a second paper or mixed set

Complete a shorter timed set, focusing on pacing. Note your checkpoint strategy: for a two-hour exam, where should you be after 30, 60 and 90 minutes?

By the end of Day 5, every major topic should have been retrieved at least twice, and every serious error should have a written correction.

---

## Day 6: Run a Full Mock Exam

**Goal:** Rehearse the event, not just the subject.

Recreate the conditions as closely as you reasonably can:

- Start at the same time of day as the real exam, if possible.
- Sit at a desk, not in bed.
- Use only permitted materials.
- Follow the full time limit.
- Take breaks only if the real exam allows them.

Mark the mock immediately. Then ask three questions:

1. Which topics still cost marks?
2. Which errors are new, and which are repeats from Day 4?
3. What will I do differently in the first 15 minutes of the real exam?

Repeated errors deserve your final study blocks. Write the correction as an instruction you can act on: “Check whether the question asks for net or total ATP” is useful. “Be careful” is not.

Create a final one-page memory sheet **from memory**: key formulas, distinctions, dates, processes or arguments. Compare it with your notes and add only genuine gaps. This sheet is for Day 7 review, not a substitute for the previous six days.

---

## Day 7: Taper, Don’t Cram

**Goal:** Arrive with a clear head, not a head stuffed five minutes before the door opens.

Keep the day deliberately light:

- Review the one-page memory sheet in two or three short sessions.
- Retest the questions you previously missed.
- Talk through the hardest explanations once, out loud.
- Stop studying early enough to eat properly and wind down.

### Pack and check

- Student ID and required stationery or calculator
- Permitted formula sheet or materials
- Water bottle
- Exam room, building and start time
- Alarm set, with travel time and a buffer
- Clothes and bag ready for the morning

Sleep is part of revision. Memory consolidation and attention both suffer when you trade the night for one more pass through notes you have already tested all week.

---

## If You Have Two Exams in the Same Week

Do not run two complete plans in parallel at full intensity. Alternate the subjects in daily blocks and protect one retrieval session for each:

- **Morning:** Exam A practice and repair
- **Afternoon:** Exam B past paper or timed set
- **Evening:** Short mixed recall for whichever exam comes first

As the first exam approaches, give it the larger share. After it is finished, switch the full plan to the second exam using the topic inventory and error log you have already built.

If both exams are on the same day, Day 6 should include a break between two shorter simulations, not two full papers back to back. Fatigue data is useful; destroying Day 7 is not.
